How to Increase Your Online Store's Sales in 2026

Blank Studio • 5 min read

In this article

  • AI personalisation sells more
  • Trust sells: the power of reviews
  • Simplify checkout, remove friction
  • Product pages that convert
  • Testing and optimising continuously

Launching an online store is now the easy part. The hard part is selling consistently, in a market where consumer attention is more divided than ever and competition is global. In 2026, growing an online store's sales no longer depends solely on more traffic or more promotions — it depends on removing friction at every stage of the buying journey and using the right tools to do it. Here's what actually makes a difference this year.

AI personalisation sells more

Artificial intelligence has stopped being a technological novelty and become a concrete competitive advantage in e-commerce. Stores using personalised product recommendations and smart search (that understands intent, not just exact keywords) see revenue increases of around 40%. The impact is even more dramatic when AI chat assistants are introduced into the buying process: they can quadruple a session's conversion rate, reaching 12.3% — compared to sessions without that support.

This happens because AI solves, in real time, the biggest obstacle facing any online store: helping the customer find exactly what they're looking for, answering questions on the spot, and suggesting relevant alternatives — instead of leaving shoppers to browse a large catalogue alone, looking for something that might not even exist.

Trust sells: the power of reviews

Buying online remains, in many ways, an act of faith — the customer can't touch the product or speak face to face with the seller. That's why reviews from other buyers have become one of the most powerful decision factors: 98% of consumers read reviews before completing a purchase. Displaying those reviews prominently — on product pages, but also on category pages and at checkout — can increase conversions by up to 270%.

Beyond written reviews, it's worth investing in visible trust signals: clear return policies, satisfaction guarantees, and transparent information about delivery times and costs. Each of these elements removes a small doubt that, added to the others, is often what leads a customer to abandon their cart.

Simplify checkout, remove friction

Checkout is the most critical moment of the entire journey — and also the most fragile. Every unnecessary field on the form, every extra step, is an opportunity for the customer to give up. The stores that convert best are the ones that trim checkout down to the essentials, offer the most popular payment methods (including local options and digital wallets), and make it clear, at every step, that the purchase is secure.

  • Reduce the number of required fields and allow guest checkout, without forcing account creation.
  • Show the total cost (shipping included) as early as possible, to avoid last-minute surprises.
  • Include security badges and visible return policies right next to the checkout button.

Product pages that convert

Before reaching checkout, customers need to feel confident about what they're buying — and that happens on the product page. High-quality images and videos, showing the product from multiple angles and in real-world use, dramatically reduce uncertainty. Descriptions should focus on benefits for the buyer (what this product solves, how it improves everyday life) rather than just cold technical specifications.

A clear, visible call-to-action button — "Add to Cart", "Buy Now" — completes the experience. It sounds like a small detail, but buttons that are hard to spot or use ambiguous wording are responsible for hesitations that ultimately translate into lost sales.

Testing and optimising continuously

Despite all these strategies being well known, only 31% of online stores have a structured approach to testing and optimisation. Most are still "guessing" what works, instead of systematically testing different versions of pages, copy, and checkout flows. Optimising conversion rate isn't a project you finish — it's an ongoing discipline that requires measuring, testing, and adjusting month after month.
